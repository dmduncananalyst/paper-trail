California Payroll Record Retention

California employers generally must keep payroll records for at least four years under Employment Development Department guidance. Federal employment tax records also generally must be kept for at least four years after the fourth-quarter return is filed for the year, according to the IRS. Different records can carry longer obligations, so a business should not treat one four-year deadline as permission to destroy every employment document.

California EDD records cover more than pay stubs

The EDD's employer guidance describes records that provide a true and accurate account of workers and payments. The information includes identifying details, dates of employment, payments, pay periods, amounts withheld and disbursement records. The EDD also notes a special eight-year retention requirement for certain household-service payment records when exemption or subject-employer status is in question. Employers should determine which rules apply to their operations rather than assuming that every business or worker arrangement is identical.

Federal employment tax documentation has its own clock

The IRS requires employment tax records to be kept for at least four years after filing the fourth-quarter return for the year. This includes information such as employee names and addresses, wage payments, amounts withheld, deposits, returns and relevant withholding forms. The IRS specifically identifies longer recordkeeping for some employment-related tax credit documentation, including certain credit records requiring at least six years. Those exceptions illustrate why a single automatic file-deletion policy can be inadequate.

Other employment rules may require separate records

Payroll taxes are not the same as every labor-law record. Timekeeping, personnel documentation, wage statements, leave records and records involved in an investigation or dispute may carry separate obligations. A record may also need to be held longer if a claim, audit, litigation hold or other retention requirement applies. A responsible policy identifies the record type and relevant law before assigning an expiration date. Businesses with unusual circumstances should seek professional guidance rather than relying on a short summary article.

Electronic storage must preserve usable information

Digital filing can support retrieval, but the system must keep records accurate, accessible and protected from inappropriate access. Payroll documents may include addresses, Social Security numbers, compensation details and banking information. Those values should not be placed in public repositories or freely shared folders. The IRS explains that electronic recordkeeping must preserve legible, accessible documentation. A folder name alone is not a compliance system if its contents cannot be retrieved or understood later.

Establish responsibilities before discarding documents

A business should know who gathers payroll records, where they are stored, which person controls access and how disposal is approved. The policy should prevent accidental deletion when an employee leaves or software changes. Periodic review can reveal missing tax deposits, incomplete identifying information or gaps in the documents used by a payroll provider. Keeping records consistently throughout the year is easier than reconstructing them after a notice arrives.
