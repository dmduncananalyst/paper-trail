A Profitable Business Can Still Run Short of Cash

A profitable business can have low cash because profit and cash measure different things. Customers may not have paid yet, inventory or equipment may have absorbed money, and loan repayments or owner withdrawals may reduce cash without appearing as ordinary expenses. The accounting basis also affects when profit is reported.

Look at the collection cycle

Under accrual accounting, sales can increase profit before payment reaches the bank. If customers take longer to pay, the receivable balance grows while available cash stays tight. Review invoice aging, disputed amounts and how promptly invoices are issued. A strong sales month does not necessarily create a strong collection month.

Track uses of cash outside current expenses

Loan principal reduces debt, and equipment purchases may create assets rather than an immediate full expense. Owner withdrawals use cash without representing the cost of serving customers. Inventory can also tie up funds before it is sold. These movements help explain why a positive profit and loss statement does not equal the cash available for the next payment.

Separate record errors from operating pressure

First reconcile accounts and check for missing deposits or duplicate payments. Then compare the profit and loss statement, balance sheet and cash flow information. A practical cash forecast adds expected collections and upcoming commitments to the historical picture. Accurate books cannot guarantee that every bill is affordable, but they make it easier to identify whether the pressure comes from collections, spending, debt payments or the timing of growth.
