The Year Ends With Gift Cards Sold, Money Collected, and Work Still Owed in January

Published 11/12/2026

This article is general information about business recordkeeping, not tax or legal advice for a specific business.

December is the one month when customers happily pay for work they do not want yet. A gift card is bought in a good mood, wrapped or emailed, and celebrated as a thoughtful present, while the appointment, the meal, the service, or the goods it promises sit weeks or months in the future. For the business on the other side of the counter, the sale feels wonderful in the moment. The register is busy, the deposits are strong, and a hard year appears to be ending on a high note that everyone earned.

That feeling is real, and it is also incomplete. The money has arrived, but the business has not finished with it. Every card sold and not yet redeemed is a promise walking around in someone else pocket, waiting to come back at a time the business does not choose. When January arrives, quieter, colder, with rent and payroll at their full size, those promises start returning one by one, asking for labor, time, and product, without bringing any new money in with them.

If the books treated December as if all of that cash were already earned, the new year opens with a distortion nobody intended, and the owner is left wondering why a strong December produced such a strained January.

Cash in hand is not the same as work completed

The confusion is understandable because, from the bank account point of view, a gift card sale looks like any other sale. Money lands, the day total rises, the month closes stronger. Nothing in a deposit announces that part of it belongs to work still owed. Only the records around the sale carry that fact, the card number, the amount, the date sold, and later, the date and amount redeemed.

The IRS recordkeeping guidance stresses that a business needs records that support the income it reports, along with gross receipts, sales records, and supporting documents that show what was received and when. Gift cards stretch that discipline across a year boundary. The receipt happens in one year, the service or goods are delivered in another, and a record that only captures the cash will misdescribe both years. December looks richer than the work it actually completed. January looks poorer, carrying the cost of fulfilling promises while showing little of the money that paid for them.

Owners feel this first as a mood, before they see it as a number. January staff are busy redeeming, the schedule may even look healthy, and yet the account does not grow the way a busy schedule should make it grow. The work is real and the money is not new. A team that celebrated December as a breakthrough month starts the year feeling behind, without any single mistake they can point to.

That gap in understanding hardens when some cards are redeemed in part, some in full, some for more than the card value with a second payment added, and some not at all for many months. Each path leaves a slightly different trace in the sales system, in the card processor reports, and in the bank. A reader later, an accountant closing the year, a buyer looking at seasonal performance, a partner trying to understand December, has to reconcile all of those traces into one honest account of what was sold, what was delivered, and what is still owed to customers holding unredeemed balances.

A liability that walks out the door in an envelope

Most obligations of a business stay visible. A vendor bill sits in a pile or a system, a loan statement arrives every month, payroll announces itself on a schedule. An unredeemed gift card is quieter. It leaves the building in an envelope, a wallet, or an inbox, and the business may not hear from it again until the least convenient week of winter, or the following holiday season, or never within the period anyone was watching.

That quietness is what lets the balance owed to card holders drift out of the books entirely. If no one kept a running record that the business could trust, the amount still owed becomes an estimate offered from memory, and memory, in a business that sold cards across busy counters, holiday events, and online orders at the same time, is not a reliable ledger. Promotional cards given away in a marketing push mix with cards that were paid for. Cards sold through a third party platform settle on a different schedule than cards sold in person. A card that was refunded, replaced, or reissued can appear twice in one system and once in another.

None of this requires carelessness at the level of intent. Holiday selling is fast, seasonal help is new, and the line at the counter rewards speed over documentation. The problems are created in small, forgivable moments, a card activated without its sale being fully recorded, a manual adjustment made to keep the line moving, an online sale that landed in a report nobody checked against the bank. Each one is minor in December. Together, they decide whether the business actually knows what it owes its own customers when the year turns.

There is a customer trust side to it as well. A holder who presents a card and is told the balance cannot be verified, or shows as less than they believe, experiences that moment as the business questioning them. The record failure becomes a service failure, in public, at the counter, months after the original sale was celebrated.

The year gets closed on a number that was never quite true

Year end is when the distortion stops being a feeling and becomes a filing. A return, a financial statement, or a simple year in review built on December cash alone will overstate what that year earned from work completed and understate what the next year still has to deliver. The federal recordkeeping baseline is that records supporting income and deductions are kept until the limitations period for the return expires, generally three years from filing, and employment tax records at least four years, so a weak gift card record does not fade quickly. It sits inside the closed year, available to confuse every comparison built on top of it.

Owners comparing years feel it without naming it. One December looks like the best month the business ever had, the next December looks like a decline, when part of the difference is only when promises were counted. A lender or buyer reading those months sees momentum where there was really an obligation building, and sees a soft January where there was really work being honored. Decisions about staffing, about whether the holiday push worked, about whether to repeat a promotion, get made on a story the cash told too early.

The businesses that sell the most cards are often the ones most exposed, because the amounts are large enough to matter and the volume is large enough to outrun informal tracking. A handful of cards can live in an owner memory. Hundreds of cards, across locations, staff, and platforms, cannot. At that scale the question is no longer whether the money was collected. It plainly was. The question is whether the business can show, card by card and month by month, how much of that collected money has been earned by work actually done, and how much is still a promise outstanding.

When it cannot, January tells the truth that December deferred. The appointments arrive, the redemptions arrive, the costs arrive, and the revenue that was supposed to accompany them is already spent, counted, and closed in a year that has ended. Nothing was stolen and nothing was wasted. The year simply called money earned before the business had finished earning it, and the new year inherits the work without the recognition.

That is the quiet weight of a gift card season recorded only by its cash. The cards were sold as a sign that customers trusted the business enough to pay in advance. Honoring that trust, on paper as well as at the counter, is what separates a strong December from a December that only looked strong until January asked for the rest of the story.
