Can a Monthly Review Catch Payroll and Sales Tax Gaps?

Published 11/05/2026

A profitable month can still hide tax record problems

A business owner looks at the monthly profit report and sees strong sales. Wages appear as an expense and the checking account has enough cash to cover the next payroll. It feels like a good month. But the accounting ledger may tell only part of the story if payroll taxes were withheld without a matching liability entry, or if sales tax collected at checkout was recorded as revenue.

A monthly bookkeeping review can identify warning signs in those records. It does not replace payroll processing, tax return preparation, or filing and deposit confirmations. Its value is in comparing separate sets of information before mismatches accumulate over many periods.

Payroll and sales tax each have their own reporting schedules. Those obligations do not necessarily align with the month end close. A review can catch inconsistent records, but the business and its designated payroll or tax professionals still need to meet the applicable deadlines.

Payroll errors are not always visible in the wage expense line

Employee net pay is what reaches an employee bank account. Gross wages, employee withholding, employer payroll taxes, benefits, and payroll liabilities can have different treatment in the books. Posting only the net payment as wage expense can leave costs and liabilities understated or misclassified.

A monthly review can compare payroll provider summaries with payroll expense accounts, payroll liabilities, and related bank payments. Large unexplained differences, stale liability balances, duplicate payroll entries, or missing employer tax expense may indicate something needs investigation.

The IRS explains that employers generally must deposit and report applicable employment taxes and that federal deposit schedules can differ from payroll pay frequency. A monthly review is therefore not permission to wait until month end to address a deposit that is due sooner.

Employment tax records also need to be preserved. The IRS states that employers generally must keep employment tax records for at least four years. A bookkeeping review can confirm that payroll reports and deposit confirmations are being retained without claiming to replace the employer or payroll provider.

Sales tax collected is not the same as money earned

A sale may include an amount collected for sales tax. Depending on the transaction and applicable rules, the tax collected may represent an amount owed to a taxing authority rather than business income. If all checkout receipts are categorized as revenue, a financial statement can overstate sales and fail to show the related obligation.

A review should compare the point of sale or invoicing summary with booked sales, refunds, taxable and nontaxable activity, and the account used to track sales tax liabilities. Payment processor fees and deposit timing also matter because bank deposits may not equal gross sales reported by the system.

For California businesses, the California Department of Tax and Fee Administration describes records needed to determine sales and use tax obligations, including books of account, source documents, and working papers used to prepare returns. Its return instructions also distinguish total sales from deductions for nontaxable transactions.

The precise taxability of a product or service depends on jurisdiction and the facts of the sale. A bookkeeper should not assume that every sale is taxable or that the same treatment applies across states. Unclear tax classifications should be referred to the appropriate tax professional.

What a useful monthly check compares

For payroll, the reviewer compares provider reports with general ledger activity, payroll liability balances, and available evidence of tax deposits. Unexplained amounts are assigned for follow up. For sales tax, the reviewer compares sales systems and accounting totals, reviews the recorded liability and available filing support, and documents items that cannot be reconciled.

The reviewer should also consider timing. Payroll may be processed near the end of a month and settled in the following month. Sales collected late in the month may reach the bank later. A difference created by timing is not necessarily an error, but it still needs an explanation that makes sense in the records.

The most useful product of the review is a short list of discrepancies, supporting documents, responsible parties, and corrective steps. That gives the owner something to act on rather than a financial report that looks polished but leaves unresolved balances hidden.

The limits of a monthly review

A monthly review cannot guarantee that every payroll tax payment was filed correctly, that every sales tax rate is accurate, or that a future audit will find no problems. Those judgments require checking the relevant filings, provider responsibilities, jurisdictional rules, and source documents.

It also cannot make incomplete source records accurate. If the business has not provided payroll statements, point of sale exports, or relevant tax account information, the analysis must disclose the missing evidence.
