South Dakota Bookkeeping

South Dakota tax bookkeeping may involve retail sales, municipal taxes, and separate contractor excise tax records. Accurate bookkeeping connects transactions to the invoices, receipts, bank activity, payroll records, and tax filings that rely on them. The details below explain the records that matter in South Dakota.

Keeping records that support the books

South Dakota requires businesses that are liable for sales, use, and related excise taxes to keep records that support the tax reported. The Department of Revenue audit page describes records tied to gross receipts, deductions, and exemptions, and cites Administrative Rule 64:06:01:35 for production of records in an examination. Source: South Dakota Department of Revenue, Audit, https://dor.sd.gov/businesses/audit/

Businesses keep a general ledger, sales journal, bank statements, and deposit records that reconcile to the sales tax return. The Department list in the audit source includes ledgers, cash register tapes, bank deposit slips and statements, sales journals, and contracts as gross receipts support.

How state taxes affect the ledger

South Dakota does not impose a state income tax. The Department of Revenue states this on its individual taxes page and in other Department publications, including that South Dakota does not impose a personal or corporate income tax. Sources: https://dor.sd.gov/individuals/taxes and https://Dor.sd.gov/media/alkhwrgn/campaign.pdf

South Dakota imposes a state sales tax and a use tax on taxable products and services. The Department sales and use tax guide lists the state rate at 4.2 percent, municipal sales and use tax at 1 to 2 percent where a municipality imposes it, a municipal gross receipts tax at 1 percent on specified categories including alcoholic beverages, eating establishments, lodging, and admissions, a contractors excise tax at 2 percent on gross receipts of prime contractors for construction and realty improvement, a motor vehicle excise tax at 4 percent, and a tourism tax at 1.5 percent on specified lodging and amusement categories. Source: https://dor.sd.gov/media/kavh1fzg/2026-1_sales-use-tax-guide.pdf

Sellers and contractors need records that separate state tax, municipal tax, municipal gross receipts tax, tourism tax, and contractors excise tax where more than one applies to different lines of business, because the taxes are reported on different bases.

Payroll records and employer reporting

South Dakota has no state personal income tax withholding for wages, because it has no personal income tax, as confirmed in the Department source in the state tax section. Employers still have federal withholding duties, which are outside this state summary.

Employers covered by reemployment assistance, the South Dakota name for unemployment insurance, must register, file quarterly wage reports, and pay contributions through the Department of Labor and Regulation. The specific employer handbook pages were not opened for this batch, so the quarterly detail is stated at this general level only.

Income, purchases, and documentation

For sales and use tax, deduction and exemption support includes resale certificates, proof of exemption, bills of lading or other proof of delivery, credit memorandums, and bad debt records as claimed on federal returns, according to the Department audit list. Source: https://dor.sd.gov/businesses/audit/

How long to retain records

All businesses should maintain sales and use tax records for at least the current month and the previous 36 months, according to the Department of Revenue audit page, which also says records requested in an examination must be provided within 60 days of the audit commencement date under the cited administrative rule. Source: https://dor.sd.gov/businesses/audit/

When an industry needs additional records

Construction. Prime contractors are subject to the contractors excise tax at 2 percent on gross receipts, so contract amounts, change orders, and owner payments need separate tracking from ordinary retail sales. Source: Department guide in the state tax section.

Lodging, eating establishments, admissions, and visitor intensive businesses can be subject to municipal gross receipts tax and tourism tax in addition to sales tax, as listed in the state tax section, so point of sale categories need to map to each tax.

Bookkeeping in practice

The following situations illustrate how a business might organize its records. They are examples, not a determination of tax treatment for any particular business.

A Sioux Falls retailer keeps sales invoices, register tapes, bank deposits, resale certificates, and its sales tax workpapers for the current month and the previous 36 months, so a Department auditor request can be answered within the 60 day window described in the retention section.

A prime contractor working on a realty improvement project tracks contract receipts separately from its retail counter sales, because the receipts may be subject to contractors excise tax rather than only retail sales tax.

Contractors excise tax and tourism activity

South Dakota construction businesses should preserve contract receipts and change orders separately from ordinary retail sales. Tourism operators can face another set of tax categories, making point of sale department codes more important than a single daily deposit number.

This is an accounting workflow distinction, not a claim that every business in South Dakota is subject to the same tax or filing rule. The applicable treatment depends on the actual transaction and the business activity. A bookkeeper's responsibility is to preserve the transaction evidence and classify the amounts consistently. Determining a disputed tax position or filing treatment is a separate professional judgment.

A practical monthly review for South Dakota businesses

Begin with the original source rather than the bank feed. A customer invoice, sales report, payroll register, supplier bill, or contract explains what occurred. Match it to the corresponding deposit or payment, investigate differences, and record adjustments with a clear explanation. When a state tax or payroll report is required, maintain a separate reconciliation from the ledger to the amounts included in that report. That allows an owner to compare operating results without confusing business income with taxes collected, employee withholdings, or other liabilities.

For an owner preparing to hand records to a tax professional, the useful package includes a period summary, filed return copies where applicable, the transactions supporting material adjustments, and a short list of unresolved questions. A reconciled bank account is only part of that package. The quality of the records depends on whether a reviewer can trace important figures back to their sources.

What to keep available

Maintain organized copies of the invoices, receipts, bank and card statements, payroll reports, account reconciliations, and any exemption or special program documents relevant to the business. A retention schedule should identify which law or program controls each category instead of assuming one deadline covers every record. Keep documents connected to a pending audit, claim, or dispute until the matter is resolved, and verify any destruction decision against the current applicable rule.
