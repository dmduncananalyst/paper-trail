New York Bookkeeping

New York sales tax records should preserve a clear audit trail, including transactions, voids, and purchasing evidence. Accurate bookkeeping connects transactions to the invoices, receipts, bank activity, payroll records, and tax filings that rely on them. The details below explain the records that matter in New York.

Keeping records that support the books

Registered sales tax vendors must keep accurate and complete records of sales and purchases. The Tax Department bulletin on recordkeeping for sales tax vendors requires records that show gross sales, taxable and nontaxable sales, purchases, and the tax collected, and it requires that records be available to the Department on request. Source: http://www.tax.ny.gov/pubs_and_bulls/tg_bulletins/st/record-keeping_requirements_for_sales_tax_vendors.htm

For income tax, the Department advises that records and supporting documents be kept for at least 3 years after a return is filed, documenting sources of income, withholding and estimated payments, and deductible expenses. Source: http://www.tax.ny.gov/pit/letters/recordkeeping.htm

How state taxes affect the ledger

New York has a state sales and use tax, a personal income tax, a corporation franchise tax, and employer withholding. Local sales taxes, including county, city, and certain district taxes, are collected with the state tax, so records must show the place of delivery or use to support the combined rate applied.

Retailers keep exemption certificates and resale certificates on file for sales treated as exempt, because the vendor bears the burden of showing why tax was not collected.

Payroll records and employer reporting

Employers file quarterly combined withholding, wage reporting, and unemployment insurance returns with the Tax Department. Unemployment insurance contributions and wage reporting are administered with the Department of Labor.

Income, purchases, and documentation

Sales tax vendor records are specified by the Tax Department and include the day to day documents that support sales and purchases. The bulletin describes records such as sales slips, invoices, receipts, purchase invoices, and exemption documents as part of the records a vendor must keep and produce. Source: http://www.tax.ny.gov/pubs_and_bulls/tg_bulletins/st/record-keeping_requirements_for_sales_tax_vendors.htm

How long to retain records

Sales tax vendors must keep all records for a minimum of 3 years from the due date of the return to which the records relate, or the date the return is filed, if later. The Department may require a longer period where records are the subject of an audit, court case, or other proceeding. Source: http://www.tax.ny.gov/pubs_and_bulls/tg_bulletins/st/record-keeping_requirements_for_sales_tax_vendors.htm

Point of sale data that is removed from a system for capacity reasons must still be transferred and kept available in a form that can be audited for the same 3 year period, and data must be transferred when a business changes point of sale systems. Source: http://www.tax.ny.gov/pubs_and_bulls/tg_bulletins/st/record-keeping_requirements_for_sales_tax_vendors.htm

When an industry needs additional records

Businesses that sell motor fuel, tobacco, alcohol, and certain other regulated products face separate excise tax registrations, returns, and product records in New York. Those duties apply only to businesses in those trades.

Healthcare providers that participate in Medicaid must keep records that support claims and payments under program rules, in addition to tax records. This applies to enrolled providers, not to all healthcare businesses.

Bookkeeping in practice

The following situations illustrate how a business might organize its records. They are examples, not a determination of tax treatment for any particular business.

A clothing shop in Albany keeps daily register totals, the point of sale audit trail, and resale certificates for wholesale customers in one monthly file. After 3 years from the filing date of a quarterly sales tax return, the shop reviews that file before destruction and keeps it if an audit is open.

A Manhattan services firm with employees who live in New York City keeps city withholding separate in its payroll register from state withholding, so the quarterly combined return and the city related wage records reconcile.

Point of sale audit trails and city obligations

A New York shop with frequent refunds, voids, and exchanges should be able to explain why its daily register total differs from gross transaction activity. Businesses in New York City may also face additional reporting categories that should not be confused with statewide obligations.

This is an accounting workflow distinction, not a claim that every business in New York is subject to the same tax or filing rule. The applicable treatment depends on the actual transaction and the business activity. A bookkeeper's responsibility is to preserve the transaction evidence and classify the amounts consistently. Determining a disputed tax position or filing treatment is a separate professional judgment.

A practical monthly review for New York businesses

Begin with the original source rather than the bank feed. A customer invoice, sales report, payroll register, supplier bill, or contract explains what occurred. Match it to the corresponding deposit or payment, investigate differences, and record adjustments with a clear explanation. When a state tax or payroll report is required, maintain a separate reconciliation from the ledger to the amounts included in that report. That allows an owner to compare operating results without confusing business income with taxes collected, employee withholdings, or other liabilities.

For an owner preparing to hand records to a tax professional, the useful package includes a period summary, filed return copies where applicable, the transactions supporting material adjustments, and a short list of unresolved questions. A reconciled bank account is only part of that package. The quality of the records depends on whether a reviewer can trace important figures back to their sources.

What to keep available

Maintain organized copies of the invoices, receipts, bank and card statements, payroll reports, account reconciliations, and any exemption or special program documents relevant to the business. A retention schedule should identify which law or program controls each category instead of assuming one deadline covers every record. Keep documents connected to a pending audit, claim, or dispute until the matter is resolved, and verify any destruction decision against the current applicable rule.
