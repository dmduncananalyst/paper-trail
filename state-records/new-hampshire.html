New Hampshire Bookkeeping

New Hampshire businesses need to distinguish profits tax records from Business Enterprise Tax records involving compensation, interest, and dividends. Accurate bookkeeping connects transactions to the invoices, receipts, bank activity, payroll records, and tax filings that rely on them. The details below explain the records that matter in New Hampshire.

Keeping records that support the books

New Hampshire business tax record duties are tax specific. For Meals and Rentals Tax operators, Department of Revenue Administration rule REV 708.01 and Revised Statutes Annotated 78-A:19 require operators to maintain records, and the Department Meals and Rentals booklet states that operators must maintain records for three years, and that if records are not adequate to make an accounting to the state for tax collection liability, the Department shall determine liability based on records available and deny the 3 percent commission available under RSA 78-A:7, III. Source: 2025 Meals and Rentals Tax Booklet, New Hampshire Department of Revenue Administration PDF

How state taxes affect the ledger

The business taxes that drive most New Hampshire bookkeeping are the Business Profits Tax and the Business Enterprise Tax. The Department states that the Business Enterprise Tax is assessed on the taxable enterprise value tax base, which is the sum of all compensation paid or accrued, interest paid or accrued, and dividends paid, at 0.55 percent for taxable periods ending on or after December 31, 2022, and that for periods beginning on or after January 1, 2025, every business enterprise with more than 298,000 dollars of gross receipts from all activities, or an enterprise value tax base of more than 298,000 dollars, must file a Business Enterprise Tax return. Business Enterprise Tax paid may be used as a credit against Business Profits Tax, with a ten taxable period carryforward for credits for periods ending on or after December 31, 2014. Source: Business Taxes, New Hampshire Department of Revenue Administration

The same Department page states that the Business Profits Tax rate is 7.5 percent for taxable periods ending on or after December 31, 2023, that multistate income is apportioned using a single sales factor for periods ending on or after December 31, 2022, that unitary businesses must use combined reporting, and that for periods beginning on or after January 1, 2025, every business organization with gross business income of more than 109,000 dollars must file a Business Profits Tax return. Source, same Department page as above. Because the Business Enterprise Tax base is compensation, interest, and dividends, payroll and interest records are tax records in New Hampshire in a way they are not in a pure profits tax state.

Payroll records and employer reporting

New Hampshire has no state income tax withholding on wages, because it has no general personal income tax on wages as described in the state tax section. Employers therefore do not keep a state wage withholding file comparable to other states in this batch, but they do keep Business Enterprise Tax compensation records, because compensation is part of that tax base, per the state tax section.

When an industry needs additional records

Restaurants, hotels, and motor vehicle rental operators are Meals and Rentals Tax operators with licensing, monthly collection and remittance, record, and commission rules described in Parts 1 and 2. Operators must file a Meals and Rentals return and pay over collected tax monthly, generally by the 15th day of the month following the taxable period, as described in Department technical information summarized in search results. The monthly due date statement is based on that Department release summary. Source: Technical Information Release, New Hampshire Department of Revenue Administration PDF

Bookkeeping in practice

The following situations illustrate how a business might organize its records. They are examples, not a determination of tax treatment for any particular business.

A New Hampshire restaurant has no general sales tax to collect on ordinary retail sales, but as a Meals and Rentals operator it collects the Meals and Rentals Tax from patrons, files monthly, keeps operator records for three years, and keeps the records adequate to account for the tax or risks losing the 3 percent collection commission described in the recordkeeping section.

A business with large payroll but low profit still analyzes Business Enterprise Tax, because that tax base includes compensation paid or accrued even when profits are low, and it keeps payroll, interest, and dividend records together with its Business Profits Tax file for that reason.

Business Enterprise Tax records

New Hampshire Business Enterprise Tax makes compensation, interest, and dividends relevant in a way that ordinary profit reports may not fully explain. Those categories should be supported and reconciled separately when the business is subject to that tax.

This is an accounting workflow distinction, not a claim that every business in New Hampshire is subject to the same tax or filing rule. The applicable treatment depends on the actual transaction and the business activity. A bookkeeper's responsibility is to preserve the transaction evidence and classify the amounts consistently. Determining a disputed tax position or filing treatment is a separate professional judgment.

A practical monthly review for New Hampshire businesses

Begin with the original source rather than the bank feed. A customer invoice, sales report, payroll register, supplier bill, or contract explains what occurred. Match it to the corresponding deposit or payment, investigate differences, and record adjustments with a clear explanation. When a state tax or payroll report is required, maintain a separate reconciliation from the ledger to the amounts included in that report. That allows an owner to compare operating results without confusing business income with taxes collected, employee withholdings, or other liabilities.

For an owner preparing to hand records to a tax professional, the useful package includes a period summary, filed return copies where applicable, the transactions supporting material adjustments, and a short list of unresolved questions. A reconciled bank account is only part of that package. The quality of the records depends on whether a reviewer can trace important figures back to their sources.

What to keep available

Maintain organized copies of the invoices, receipts, bank and card statements, payroll reports, account reconciliations, and any exemption or special program documents relevant to the business. A retention schedule should identify which law or program controls each category instead of assuming one deadline covers every record. Keep documents connected to a pending audit, claim, or dispute until the matter is resolved, and verify any destruction decision against the current applicable rule.
