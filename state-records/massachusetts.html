Massachusetts Bookkeeping

Massachusetts requires accurate and accessible tax records, including electronic transaction data where used. Accurate bookkeeping connects transactions to the invoices, receipts, bank activity, payroll records, and tax filings that rely on them. The details below explain the records that matter in Massachusetts.

Keeping records that support the books

Massachusetts regulation 830 CMR 62C.25.1 requires every person subject to tax under the listed Massachusetts tax chapters, or liable to collect such a tax, to preserve permanent books of account or records that are sufficiently accurate and complete to establish gross income, deductions, credits, and other matters required to be shown in a return. Records must be easily locatable and organized so the Commissioner of Revenue can determine liability, and machine sensible records must contain transaction level detail and be retrievable in a standard format on request. Failure to maintain complete, accurate, and accessible records can lead the Commissioner to determine tax from other information and can lead to penalties, including disallowance of deductions, credits, and exemptions. Source: 830 CMR 62C.25.1, Mass.gov

Businesses keep a general ledger tied to source documents, bank records, and filed returns, because the regulation makes the taxpayer bear the burden of proof for entries, deductions, and credits claimed. That practice statement follows from the burden rule in the same regulation cited above.

How state taxes affect the ledger

Massachusetts taxes that commonly drive bookkeeping include personal income tax and withholding on wages, corporate excise, sales and use tax, sales tax on meals, room occupancy excise, and marijuana retail taxes. The record retention regulation expressly covers chapters that include income tax, corporate and business excises, sales and use tax, and Paid Family and Medical Leave contributions under chapter 175M. Sources: 830 CMR 62C.25.1, Mass.gov

Payroll records and employer reporting

Unemployment insurance wage reporting is quarterly. The Department of Unemployment Assistance states that employment and wage detail reports are due April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31, and that a report needs the employer account number, employee Social Security number and full name, hours worked, gross wages, wages subject to withholding, state income tax withheld, work location, and 12th of the month employment data. Source: Submit employment and wage detail report, Mass.gov

Income, purchases, and documentation

The retention regulation requires records sufficient to support gross income, deductions, and credits, and its electronic data interchange section gives examples of the detail an acceptable record carries, including vendor and purchaser name, invoice date, product description, quantity, price, amount of tax, tax status, and shipping details. Point of sale records must permit direct reconciliation of receipts and invoices with books and returns. Source: 830 CMR 62C.25.1, Mass.gov

How long to retain records

Records required by 830 CMR 62C.25.1 must be kept as long as their contents are material to Massachusetts tax administration. At a minimum, unless the Commissioner consents in writing to earlier destruction, records must be preserved until the statute of limitations for additional assessments for the period expires, which the regulation states is generally three years after the due date of the return or the date the return was actually filed, whichever is later. Exceptions stated in the regulation include fraud or failure to file, where assessment may occur at any time, an agreed extension, records for a future taxable event such as property basis, which must be kept until three years after the return for the year of disposition, and records supporting a refund, credit, or abatement claim, which must be kept until final resolution of the claim. Source: 830 CMR 62C.25.1, Mass.gov

When an industry needs additional records

Meals vendors have the extra daily receipts, bar check, and register tape duties described in the state tax section. Marijuana retailers have the approved point of sale and gross receipts verification duties described in the state tax section, and manipulation of point of sale data is prohibited.

Human and social service providers paid by the state are subject to state compliance, reporting, and auditing rules, including filing an annual Uniform Financial Statements and Independent Auditor Report under 808 CMR 1.00 as referenced in Executive Office of Health and Human Services rate regulations. That scope is limited to providers purchasing services through the state, not to businesses generally. Source for the reference as it appeared in a proposed regulation filing: Proposed Regulation, Mass.gov

Bookkeeping in practice

The following situations illustrate how a business might organize its records. They are examples, not a determination of tax treatment for any particular business.

A restaurant in a town that adopted the local meals excise keeps, for each day, register tapes, bar checks, dining checks, and a daily receipts record, separated into taxable meals, alcohol, and nontaxable food, and keeps the filed meals returns with those records for at least the three year assessment period described in the retention section.

A retailer destroys sales records two years after filing. Because the general minimum period runs until the assessment limitations period expires, generally three years after the due date or filing date, whichever is later, the destruction is early under the regulation cited in the retention section, even if no audit is pending.

Digital records and local meals excise activity

A restaurant sales report should distinguish meals, alcohol, and other revenue where the applicable rules require it. Massachusetts electronic records standards also make it important that old data can still be read and tied to the books after software migrations.

This is an accounting workflow distinction, not a claim that every business in Massachusetts is subject to the same tax or filing rule. The applicable treatment depends on the actual transaction and the business activity. A bookkeeper's responsibility is to preserve the transaction evidence and classify the amounts consistently. Determining a disputed tax position or filing treatment is a separate professional judgment.

A practical monthly review for Massachusetts businesses

Begin with the original source rather than the bank feed. A customer invoice, sales report, payroll register, supplier bill, or contract explains what occurred. Match it to the corresponding deposit or payment, investigate differences, and record adjustments with a clear explanation. When a state tax or payroll report is required, maintain a separate reconciliation from the ledger to the amounts included in that report. That allows an owner to compare operating results without confusing business income with taxes collected, employee withholdings, or other liabilities.

For an owner preparing to hand records to a tax professional, the useful package includes a period summary, filed return copies where applicable, the transactions supporting material adjustments, and a short list of unresolved questions. A reconciled bank account is only part of that package. The quality of the records depends on whether a reviewer can trace important figures back to their sources.

What to keep available

Maintain organized copies of the invoices, receipts, bank and card statements, payroll reports, account reconciliations, and any exemption or special program documents relevant to the business. A retention schedule should identify which law or program controls each category instead of assuming one deadline covers every record. Keep documents connected to a pending audit, claim, or dispute until the matter is resolved, and verify any destruction decision against the current applicable rule.
