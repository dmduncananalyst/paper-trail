Ohio Bookkeeping

Ohio businesses may need to account separately for sales and use tax, municipal withholding, and commercial activity tax receipts. Accurate bookkeeping connects transactions to the invoices, receipts, bank activity, payroll records, and tax filings that rely on them. The details below explain the records that matter in Ohio.

Keeping records that support the books

Ohio law requires sellers and users subject to sales and use tax to keep records, receipts, invoices, bills of lading, asset ledgers, depreciation schedules, transfer journals, and other primary and secondary records in the form the Tax Commissioner requires. Those records must be open to inspection by the Commissioner during business hours. Source: http://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5741.15

For income tax, the Tax Commissioner may prescribe requirements for keeping records and other pertinent documents and for filing copies of federal income tax returns and determinations, and may require a person to keep records that show whether the person is liable for tax or for withholding, and the extent of that liability. Source: https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5747.17

How state taxes affect the ledger

Ohio has a state sales and use tax, a personal income tax, employer withholding, and a commercial activity tax. County and transit authority sales taxes add to the state sales tax rate, so sales records must show the county of delivery.

Because the CAT is based on gross receipts rather than profit, businesses keep a receipts reconciliation that starts with total receipts in the books, removes receipts sourced outside Ohio and excluded receipts, and ends with Ohio taxable gross receipts for the CAT return.

Payroll records and employer reporting

Employers withhold Ohio income tax and file withholding returns and annual reconciliation with the Department of Taxation. Employers also report wages quarterly for unemployment insurance to the Ohio Department of Job and Family Services.

Income, purchases, and documentation

The use tax record rule in the recordkeeping section of this state section names the documents to be kept, including invoices, bills of lading, asset ledgers, and depreciation schedules, so purchase invoices and asset records in Ohio are expressly tax records. Source: http://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5741.15

How long to retain records

Sales and use tax records under Ohio Revised Code the retention section741.15 must be preserved for a period of 4 years, unless the Tax Commissioner consents in writing to destruction within that period or orders that they be kept longer. Source: http://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5741.15

Income tax records under Ohio Revised Code the retention section747.17 must also be preserved for a period of 4 years, with the same written consent and longer order exceptions. Source: https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5747.17

When an industry needs additional records

Ohio severance tax applies to oil, gas, coal, and other natural resources severed in the state, and severers keep production and sales records for that tax. This applies only to severers and related purchasers.

Motor fuel, cigarette, and alcohol businesses face separate excise tax licences, returns, and product records in Ohio.

Bookkeeping in practice

The following situations illustrate how a business might organize its records. They are examples, not a determination of tax treatment for any particular business.

A Columbus distributor buys equipment from an out of state vendor that charged no tax. The bookkeeper records the purchase in a use tax file with the invoice and the asset ledger entry, accrues Ohio use tax, and keeps the file for 4 years from the return for that period.

A growing Ohio retailer crosses into CAT filing. At year end it prepares a receipts reconciliation that separates Ohio receipts from receipts sourced to other states and keeps the invoices and sales records behind the reconciliation for at least 4 years after the CAT return is filed.

Commercial Activity Tax and city payroll taxation

Ohio businesses need to distinguish sales tax collected from customers from their own gross receipts based tax obligations. Employers operating in multiple municipalities also benefit from accurate work location data, because a single state withholding total may not explain local obligations.

This is an accounting workflow distinction, not a claim that every business in Ohio is subject to the same tax or filing rule. The applicable treatment depends on the actual transaction and the business activity. A bookkeeper's responsibility is to preserve the transaction evidence and classify the amounts consistently. Determining a disputed tax position or filing treatment is a separate professional judgment.

A practical monthly review for Ohio businesses

Begin with the original source rather than the bank feed. A customer invoice, sales report, payroll register, supplier bill, or contract explains what occurred. Match it to the corresponding deposit or payment, investigate differences, and record adjustments with a clear explanation. When a state tax or payroll report is required, maintain a separate reconciliation from the ledger to the amounts included in that report. That allows an owner to compare operating results without confusing business income with taxes collected, employee withholdings, or other liabilities.

For an owner preparing to hand records to a tax professional, the useful package includes a period summary, filed return copies where applicable, the transactions supporting material adjustments, and a short list of unresolved questions. A reconciled bank account is only part of that package. The quality of the records depends on whether a reviewer can trace important figures back to their sources.

What to keep available

Maintain organized copies of the invoices, receipts, bank and card statements, payroll reports, account reconciliations, and any exemption or special program documents relevant to the business. A retention schedule should identify which law or program controls each category instead of assuming one deadline covers every record. Keep documents connected to a pending audit, claim, or dispute until the matter is resolved, and verify any destruction decision against the current applicable rule.
