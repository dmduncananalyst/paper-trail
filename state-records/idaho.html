Idaho Bookkeeping

Idaho sales tax documentation connects customer exemptions, purchases, and use tax to the ledger. Accurate bookkeeping connects transactions to the invoices, receipts, bank activity, payroll records, and tax filings that rely on them. The details below explain the records that matter in Idaho.

Keeping records that support the books

Idaho requires businesses that collect or owe sales and use tax to keep records that show they properly collected, reported, and paid tax. The Idaho State Tax Commission states that records must include normal books of account and documents that support entries in those books, including bills, receipts, invoices, cash register tapes, job or work orders, contracts, and all schedules or working papers used to prepare tax returns. Sources: Idaho State Tax Commission, Lodging Recordkeeping, https://tax.idaho.gov/taxes/sales-use/guides-for-certain-groups/lodging/record-keeping/ and Wholesalers Recordkeeping, https://tax.idaho.gov/taxes/sales-use/guides-for-certain-groups/wholesalers/record-keeping/

How state taxes affect the ledger

Idaho has a state sales tax and a complementary use tax. The state rate is 6 percent. There is no general local sales tax imposed by most cities in Idaho, although certain resort cities and auditorium districts can impose local option taxes on specific categories such as lodging, liquor by the drink, and retail sales in resort cities. Bookkeeping must therefore identify sales by location and category where a local option tax may apply.

Idaho also has individual and corporate income taxes and employer withholding. Businesses also encounter travel and convention tax and lodging taxes on short term stays, and excise taxes on specific products. Idaho has no gross receipts tax on businesses generally.

Payroll records and employer reporting

Unemployment insurance and withholding. Employers register with the Idaho Department of Labor for unemployment insurance, file quarterly wage reports, and pay contributions, and register with the State Tax Commission for income tax withholding. Payroll books must track wages by employee by quarter, withholding, and taxable wage amounts.

When an industry needs additional records

Lodging. Hotels, motels, and short term rental operators have added record duties, including keeping lease agreements and related documents where a room is continuously occupied for more than 30 days, because that affects lodging tax treatment, plus records for travel and convention and local lodging taxes where they apply. Source: Tax Commission lodging recordkeeping page linked in the recordkeeping section.

Contractors. Idaho contractors are generally consumers of materials they install, so purchase invoices matter for use tax. Records must separate materials, labor, and purchases where tax was paid versus where use tax is owed.

Bookkeeping in practice

The following situations illustrate how a business might organize its records. They are examples, not a determination of tax treatment for any particular business.

A Boise wholesaler sells to a retailer who claims resale exemption. The wholesaler keeps the resale certificate, the invoice, and the delivery record for at least four years. If it cannot document why tax was not collected, the Commission can bill the wholesaler for the tax.

A small inn keeps a separate lodging ledger showing nightly charges, tax collected, and any guest who stayed more than 30 continuous days with the lease agreement on file, so its lodging and sales tax returns can be traced to source records.

Exemption documentation and lodging records

An Idaho wholesale invoice and an Idaho lodging booking produce different records. For wholesale transactions, the purchaser and exemption basis need to be traceable. For lodging, occupancy dates and the type of stay can affect the supporting tax file.

This is an accounting workflow distinction, not a claim that every business in Idaho is subject to the same tax or filing rule. The applicable treatment depends on the actual transaction and the business activity. A bookkeeper's responsibility is to preserve the transaction evidence and classify the amounts consistently. Determining a disputed tax position or filing treatment is a separate professional judgment.

A practical monthly review for Idaho businesses

Begin with the original source rather than the bank feed. A customer invoice, sales report, payroll register, supplier bill, or contract explains what occurred. Match it to the corresponding deposit or payment, investigate differences, and record adjustments with a clear explanation. When a state tax or payroll report is required, maintain a separate reconciliation from the ledger to the amounts included in that report. That allows an owner to compare operating results without confusing business income with taxes collected, employee withholdings, or other liabilities.

For an owner preparing to hand records to a tax professional, the useful package includes a period summary, filed return copies where applicable, the transactions supporting material adjustments, and a short list of unresolved questions. A reconciled bank account is only part of that package. The quality of the records depends on whether a reviewer can trace important figures back to their sources.

What to keep available

Maintain organized copies of the invoices, receipts, bank and card statements, payroll reports, account reconciliations, and any exemption or special program documents relevant to the business. A retention schedule should identify which law or program controls each category instead of assuming one deadline covers every record. Keep documents connected to a pending audit, claim, or dispute until the matter is resolved, and verify any destruction decision against the current applicable rule.
