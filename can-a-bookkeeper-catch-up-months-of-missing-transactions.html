Can a Bookkeeper Catch Up Months of Missing Transactions?

Published 11/05/2026

When months of records are missing

A company can stay busy while its bookkeeping falls behind. Customers continue paying, vendors continue sending bills, and the bank account remains active. Meanwhile, the accounting software shows only part of the story. Perhaps the bank feed stopped importing in May. Perhaps invoices were entered but card purchases were not. Perhaps the owner postponed categorizing transactions until the amount of work felt impossible.

A bookkeeper can often reconstruct missing months, but the work is not simply pressing a button to import transactions. It is a process of rebuilding an accurate record of income, expenses, transfers, liabilities, and account balances, then checking it against independent documents.

The first task is to determine the gap. Missing transactions in one account are different from an entire year in which no bank accounts, credit cards, payroll activity, or invoices were recorded.

The records that make a catch up project possible

Bank statements are the starting point for many businesses because they establish what actually cleared each account during the period. Credit card statements, payment processor reports, invoice records, receipts, loan statements, and payroll summaries add information that a bank statement alone cannot provide.

For example, a deposit shown on a bank statement might combine several customer payments after a processor deducted fees. Recording only the net deposit as sales could understate gross revenue and leave payment fees unrecorded. The bookkeeper may need the processor settlement report to separate the components correctly.

Likewise, a payment to a credit card is not necessarily a business expense. It may be a transfer that pays down a liability, while the underlying card purchases are recorded separately. Treating both as expenses could double count costs.

The IRS says business records should identify income and expenses and be supported by documents such as invoices, receipts, account statements, and other transaction evidence. Those principles apply whether entries are made promptly or reconstructed later.

How missing months are rebuilt

A structured catch up begins with the last period known to be accurate. The bookkeeper identifies every bank account, credit card, loan, and relevant payment platform, then gathers statements and existing records for the incomplete months. Transactions already in the ledger are compared against those statements to avoid adding the same event twice.

Missing transactions are entered or matched, classifications are reviewed, and transfers between business accounts are identified. Payroll entries are compared with the payroll system. Unpaid customer invoices and vendor bills are reviewed separately from the cash movement they may eventually create.

After the transaction work, each applicable bank and credit card account is reconciled in sequence. A difference in one month may carry into the next. That is why reconciling the most recent month alone may not solve a history of incomplete bookkeeping.

The final review should look at profit and loss results, the balance sheet, open receivables, payables, loan balances, and unusual account balances. A report can balance mechanically and still contain an expense coded to the wrong account or a transfer mistaken for revenue.

What happens when the owner cannot find every receipt

Missing supporting documents do not automatically make all reconstruction impossible. Statements and provider histories may help identify payments, but they do not always establish a purchase purpose or whether an item was business or personal. A bookkeeper should flag unsupported or unclear items for the owner to explain rather than guessing.

If an account statement or critical payroll record cannot be obtained, the bookkeeper may have to leave a documented exception until the source can be recovered. It is better to show what is unresolved than to present invented certainty.

Record retention requirements depend on the record and its purpose. Employment tax documentation has specific federal retention requirements, and sales tax documentation may have additional state requirements. The business should work with its tax preparer or appropriate professional where filings or deductions may be affected.

How long catch up work takes

The calendar gap is only one measure of the workload. Three months of transactions across eight accounts can require more review than six months in a business using one bank account with consistent documentation. The number of transactions, quality of source records, payroll complexity, and amount of prior reconciliation work all matter.

The deliverable should be a defined set of corrected periods and a list of any unresolved items, not an unverified promise that every historical transaction can be completed immediately.
