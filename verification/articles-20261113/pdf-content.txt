Accounts Payable and Accounts Receivable: Keeping Bills and
Customer Payments Organized
Original URL filename: accounts-payable-and-receivable.html
Canonical URL: https://www.papertrailusa.com/accounts-payable-and-receivable.html
Meta title: Accounts Payable and Accounts Receivable: Keeping Bills and Customer Payments Organized
Meta description: Learn how accurate accounts payable and receivable records support invoice tracking, vendor payments,
reconciliations, and cash planning.
Publication date: use only a verified original date; otherwise obtain an approved truthful replacement date.
Two balances that tell different stories
A company can make plenty of sales and still struggle to pay its bills on time. The missing information is often the
difference between money customers owe the business and money the business owes its vendors. Accounts
receivable tracks invoices issued to customers that remain unpaid. Accounts payable tracks approved vendor bills that
have not yet been paid. Both are important, but neither balance by itself tells you how much cash is available today.
Imagine a small service company completes $14,000 of work this month and sends invoices with payment due in 30
days. Its suppliers, insurance provider, and subcontractors expect $9,000 before those customers pay. The company
has earned revenue and created receivables, yet it still needs enough cash to cover near term obligations. That timing
gap is why orderly records matter.
Build a reliable receivables process
Issue an invoice promptly after the agreed service or milestone. Use a unique invoice number, customer name,
description of work, amount, due date, and payment instructions. Record payments against the correct invoice rather
than as unexplained deposits. Review an aging report every week so invoices that are 15, 30, or 60 days overdue are
visible.
When a customer disputes an invoice, document the issue and the resolution. Do not simply erase an unpaid balance
to make the report look cleaner. Credit memos, adjustments, and write offs should follow an approved process so the
records explain what happened.
Keep payables organized without duplicate payments
Enter vendor bills when received, attach the original invoice, check the vendor name and due date, and confirm the bill
has not already been entered. If approval is required, record who approved it. Match payments to bills and reconcile
the bank and credit card accounts. A recurring subscription should be recorded consistently, while an unusual bill
deserves closer review before payment.
Good payable records also make spending patterns visible. A business can identify late fees, repeated vendor
charges, and expenses that have grown beyond the budget.

Review upcoming customer collections alongside the next two to four weeks of bills, payroll, and other commitments.

A Bookkeeping Checklist for New Businesses Before
Transactions Pile Up
Original URL filename: bookkeeping-checklist-for-new-businesses.html
Canonical URL: https://www.papertrailusa.com/bookkeeping-checklist-for-new-businesses.html
Meta title: A Bookkeeping Checklist for New Businesses Before Transactions Pile Up
Meta description: Use this bookkeeping checklist to organize business accounts, receipts, invoices, reconciliations, and records from the
start.
Publication date: use only a verified original date; otherwise obtain an approved truthful replacement date.
Set up the foundation before the first busy month
New businesses often begin with a checking account, a payment processor, and a folder of receipts. Within weeks,
deposits, vendor payments, taxes, refunds, and owner transfers can become difficult to separate. A simple
bookkeeping setup prevents the first quarter from turning into a reconstruction project.
Start by keeping business activity separate from personal activity. Open a dedicated business bank account when
appropriate for the entity and banking requirements. Choose a bookkeeping system capable of recording income,
expenses, assets, liabilities, and owner transactions. Decide who will enter activity, how often it will be reviewed, and
where supporting documents will be stored.
Create the working checklist
Record the legal business name, entity type, tax year, business address, and relevant tax identification information in
secure administrative records. Connect each active business bank and credit card account to the bookkeeping
process. List any payment processors, financing accounts, sales platforms, and payroll systems. Set up a practical
chart of accounts that reflects what the company actually sells and spends.
Establish how invoices will be issued, how customer payments will be matched, and who approves vendor bills. Decide
how refunds, discounts, deposits, reimbursements, and owner contributions will be coded. Keep copies of contracts,
receipts, invoices, bank statements, loan documents, and asset purchase records. Make sure backup access and
document permissions are controlled.
Choose a weekly and monthly rhythm
Each week, record newly issued invoices, customer receipts, bills, purchases, and any cash transactions that the bank
feed does not explain. Review overdue invoices and scheduled payments. Check for duplicates, unrecognized
merchant names, and uncategorized transfers.
At month end, reconcile the bank and credit card balances to statements, investigate outstanding items, and review the
profit and loss statement and balance sheet. Confirm payroll and sales tax liabilities are recorded correctly when
applicable. Save a dated month end report package and note unresolved items. Do not assume a bank balance is the
same thing as profit.
Avoid an expensive first year cleanup
The IRS expects business records to support income and deductions, regardless of whether the records are on paper
or electronic. Keeping transaction evidence as the business operates is generally easier than asking owners to locate
missing receipts months later. Paper Trail can set up a consistent bookkeeping routine and help maintain organized

Five Recordkeeping Steps That Could Help a Business Avoid
Overpaying Taxes for 2025
Original URL filename: 5-ways-to-cut-your-small-business-tax-bill-in-2025.html
Canonical URL: https://www.papertrailusa.com/5-ways-to-cut-your-small-business-tax-bill-in-2025.html
Meta title: Five Recordkeeping Steps That Could Help a Business Avoid Overpaying Taxes for 2025
Meta description: Review five recordkeeping practices that help businesses document deductible expenses, payments, and financial
activity for tax preparation.
Publication date: use only a verified original date; otherwise obtain an approved truthful replacement date.
Treat this as a 2025 tax year review
This article addresses records for the 2025 tax year. Tax rules, thresholds, and eligibility can change, so a qualified tax
professional should confirm the law that applies to the business and the year of the return. Bookkeeping cannot
guarantee a tax reduction, but accurate records can help taxpayers claim deductions and credits they are legitimately
entitled to and avoid reporting errors.
1. Locate ordinary and necessary business expenses Review vendor bills, card statements, subscriptions, business
insurance, professional services, and other operating costs. The IRS generally requires deductible business expenses
to be ordinary and necessary, subject to additional restrictions and exceptions. Do not assume every bank withdrawal
is deductible. Personal purchases, debt principal, and certain assets require different treatment.
2. Reconcile payment processor deposits Credit card and online payment services may deduct processing fees or
refund amounts before sending deposits to the bank. Recording only the net deposit can leave both revenue and fees
misstated. Reconcile sales records, processor statements, chargebacks, refunds, and bank deposits to show the
underlying activity correctly.
3. Separate assets from routine expenses Equipment, furniture, computers, and certain improvements may require
capitalization, depreciation, or special treatment rather than automatic expense recognition. Keep the invoice,
purchase date, business use information, and disposal records. Your tax preparer can determine whether any
deduction election is available for the applicable year.
4. Review owner payments and reimbursements Separate owner draws, contributions, payroll, contractor payments,
and documented business reimbursements. Their treatment differs by entity and transaction. Leaving all payments in
one expense category can distort the books and the tax return. Obtain appropriate documentation before making
reclassifications.
5. Recheck estimated payments and supporting records Match tax payments to confirmation records and the correct
tax year. For businesses or owners subject to estimated tax requirements, underpayment rules may apply. The IRS
provides estimated tax
guidance, but calculation and filing decisions belong with the taxpayer and tax professional.
Paper Trail can organize 2025 transactions, reconcile accounts, and prepare complete books for a tax professional. It
does not promise a particular tax saving, and it does not replace tax advice.

IRS Publication 334 (2025), Tax Guide for business: https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334

Three Ways Better Bookkeeping Can Support Accurate Business
Taxes
Original URL filename: 3-ways-to-save-money-on-your-small-business-taxes.html
Canonical URL: https://www.papertrailusa.com/3-ways-to-save-money-on-your-small-business-taxes.html
Meta title: Three Ways Better Bookkeeping Can Support Accurate Business Taxes
Meta description: Explore how reconciled accounts, organized expense records, and reliable reports support business tax preparation.
Publication date: use only a verified original date; otherwise obtain an approved truthful replacement date.
Good tax preparation begins before the return
Businesses often think about taxes when the filing deadline approaches. By then, missing receipts and misclassified
payments can make the work slower and less reliable. Clean bookkeeping does not create deductions that do not exist,
but it can help a qualified preparer identify legitimate items and avoid costly reporting mistakes.
1. Capture business expenses with their evidence Maintain a consistent method for collecting invoices, receipts,
merchant statements, and payment confirmations. Label transactions so it is clear who was paid, what the purchase
was for, and which business activity it supported. For certain deductions, additional substantiation rules apply. A
transaction description alone may not be enough.
Separate personal spending from business expenses and track shared expenses carefully. A tax preparer should
determine what portion, if any, qualifies under the applicable rules.
2. Keep revenue and deposits correctly matched Deposits can represent customer payments, loans, owner
contributions, transfers, or refunds. Not every deposit is revenue, and the timing of income recognition depends on the
business's accounting method and applicable rules. Reconcile bank deposits with invoices, merchant statements, and
other records so the tax preparer can understand what money came in and why.
Do not erase or ignore payments that appear inconsistent. Flag unresolved items and document their eventual
resolution.
3. Review books before year end A year end review gives the business time to identify missing documents, review
outstanding invoices and bills, confirm asset purchases, and correct duplicate transactions. Compare account balances
with actual statements and verify payroll or contractor information when applicable. Discuss unusual items with the
accountant before filing rather than guessing at tax treatment.
Paper Trail can maintain the records and provide organized financial reports for tax preparation. The tax professional
determines applicable deductions, credits, and filing positions, while the bookkeeping process keeps supporting
information available.

Preparing for Tax Season as a Business Owner
Original URL filename: how-to-prepare-for-tax-season-as-a-small-business-owner.html
Canonical URL: https://www.papertrailusa.com/how-to-prepare-for-tax-season-as-a-small-business-owner.html
Meta title: Preparing for Tax Season as a Business Owner
Meta description: Prepare for tax season with organized transactions, reconciled accounts, supporting documents, and reports for your
tax professional.
Publication date: use only a verified original date; otherwise obtain an approved truthful replacement date.
Prepare the records before preparing the return
Tax season becomes harder when the business has months of uncategorized transactions, old bank statements, and
missing customer records. The goal is to assemble a complete, reconciled record of business activity for the tax year. A
bookkeeper can help organize these records, while the tax preparer determines how they appear on the return.
Collect a complete account list
Identify all checking, savings, credit card, financing, merchant processing, and payment platform accounts used for
business. Include accounts opened or closed during the year. Collect statements for the entire reporting period, not just
December. Provide records for business cash transactions and transactions paid personally on behalf of the business.
The tax professional may also need ownership, entity, and prior year information. Avoid sending identification numbers
through unsecured email when a secure client portal is available.
Reconcile and classify transactions
Reconcile every bank and credit card account through year end. Review unrecognized payments, transfers between
accounts, loan proceeds, refunds, and large asset purchases. Confirm that customer payments and invoices are
matched and that unpaid vendor bills are represented correctly under the business's accounting method.
For companies with employees, collect payroll summaries, employment tax filings, and payment records. The IRS
requires employment tax records to be retained for at least four years, and some circumstances can require longer
retention.
Prepare the handoff package
A useful package includes a year end profit and loss statement, balance sheet, general ledger, bank reconciliation
summaries, asset purchases, loan information, payroll reports where applicable, and a list of unresolved transactions.
Include supporting documents for unusual expenses or transactions whose classification is uncertain.
Review the reports for obvious inconsistencies before submitting them. A negative account balance, duplicate income,
unexplained owner payments, or a bank account that does not reconcile deserves attention. Paper Trail can organize
the bookkeeping portion of this handoff so the business and tax
professional can work from the same underlying records.

What a Bookkeeper Does for Your Business
Original URL filename: what-is-a-bookkeeper-and-do-i-need-one.html
Canonical URL: https://www.papertrailusa.com/what-is-a-bookkeeper-and-do-i-need-one.html
Meta title: What a Bookkeeper Does for Your Business
Meta description: Understand a bookkeeper’s ongoing role in tracking transactions, reconciling accounts, managing invoices, and
preparing financial reports.
Publication date: use only a verified original date; otherwise obtain an approved truthful replacement date.
The role behind organized business records
A bookkeeper records and organizes financial activity so the business can understand money coming in, money going
out, and balances that still need attention. The work often includes categorizing transactions, maintaining the general
ledger, reconciling bank and credit card statements, tracking invoices and bills, and preparing routine financial reports.
Bookkeeping is not the same thing as auditing or preparing a tax return. Accountants and tax professionals may use
the bookkeeper's records for broader accounting, filing, and advisory work. The specific responsibilities depend on the
engagement and the provider's qualifications.
What regular bookkeeping looks like
During a normal month, sales and expenses are entered into the records, deposits are matched to their sources,
vendor bills are logged, and unclear transactions are flagged. At month end, balances are reconciled to outside
statements and reports are reviewed for discrepancies. The bookkeeper may also track unpaid customer invoices,
upcoming bills, and supporting documents.
A well run process makes corrections visible. Rather than masking an unreconciled amount with a generic adjustment,
the bookkeeper documents the missing information and requests clarification.
Why ongoing bookkeeping matters for every business
Every business benefits from reliable, consistently maintained financial records. Recording income and expenses,
reconciling accounts, and tracking invoices and bills are ongoing responsibilities, whether the business is new,
established, growing, or operating with a steady transaction volume.
Accurate bookkeeping also prevents avoidable backlogs. Unreviewed bank transactions, duplicate bills, overdue
invoices, and outdated reports can make it difficult to understand financial obligations and prepare accurate records for
tax season.

bill tracking, financial reporting, and historical bookkeeping repair. The work is organized around the company’s

How to Prepare Your Books for Tax Season Without Guessing at
Balances
Original URL filename: how-to-prepare-your-books-for-tax-season.html
Canonical URL: https://www.papertrailusa.com/how-to-prepare-your-books-for-tax-season.html
Meta title: How to Prepare Your Books for Tax Season Without Guessing at Balances
Meta description: Learn how to reconcile accounts, review balances, organize supporting records, and prepare a clear bookkeeping
package for tax season.
Publication date: use only a verified original date; otherwise obtain an approved truthful replacement date.
Begin with the accounts that can be verified
Before a tax preparer can rely on reports, the underlying bank, credit card, and loan balances should be reviewed.
Download the full year's statements and reconcile each account through the reporting date. Identify accounts that were
closed, payment platforms that hold balances, and transfers that may be counted twice. A report can look polished
while its balances remain wrong; reconciliation is the basic test.
Clear transaction problems
Review any uncategorized spending, unidentified deposits, duplicate invoices, unapplied customer payments,
outstanding checks, and vendor credits. Separate loan proceeds from earned revenue, and debt principal payments
from interest or other charges. Verify the purpose and documentation of large or unusual expenses.
Do not force adjustments just to make an account match. If an item cannot be substantiated, document the question
and leave a clear exception for review.
Check reports and supporting schedules
Once transactions are reconciled, review the profit and loss statement, balance sheet, and general ledger. Look for
negative balances where they do not make sense, income recorded in the wrong period, duplicated expenses, missing
liabilities, or unexplained owner activity. Create or update supporting schedules for fixed assets, loans, prepaid items,
and other material balances as applicable.
Where payroll is involved, compare payroll reports, employer tax payments, and relevant liability accounts. If inventory
is material, coordinate with the person responsible for inventory counts and the accounting method.
Send a clean, documented package
Provide your tax professional with reconciled reports and access to the documents supporting unusual transactions.
Clearly distinguish completed reconciliations from open questions. Keep a dated copy of the package and a record of
any later corrections. The IRS emphasizes that records must support items reported on tax returns.
Paper Trail can reconcile accounts, organize supporting records, and prepare routine financial statements for this
process. Determining the correct tax treatment remains the responsibility of the
business and its qualified tax adviser.

What Business Owners Need to Know About Bookkeeping
Original URL filename: what-small-business-owners-need-to-know-about-bookkeeping.html
Canonical URL: https://www.papertrailusa.com/what-small-business-owners-need-to-know-about-bookkeeping.html
Meta title: What Business Owners Need to Know About Bookkeeping
Meta description: Learn the essential bookkeeping records and routines business owners should understand, including cash flow,
invoices, bills, and reconciliations.
Publication date: use only a verified original date; otherwise obtain an approved truthful replacement date.
Bookkeeping records what is happening
Bookkeeping is the ongoing record of the financial transactions of a business. It includes customer sales, collections,
bills, purchases, bank activity, credit card activity, financing, and owner transactions. The records form the basis for
financial reports and help explain why a bank balance changed from one month to the next.
A payment received is not always new income, and a payment sent is not always a deductible operating expense.
Money may move between business accounts, represent a customer deposit, repay loan principal, or reimburse an
owner. Correct classification matters.
Know the main reports
A profit and loss statement shows revenue and expenses for a period under the accounting method used. A balance
sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific date. A cash flow view helps explain when cash is expected to
enter or leave the business. These reports answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.
The SBA identifies bookkeeping and basic financial understanding as important parts of managing a business. The
reports are only as reliable as the transactions and reconciliations behind them.
Make monthly reconciliation a standard task
Compare the books with bank and card statements and investigate differences. Check that customer payments were
applied to invoices, vendor bills were not duplicated, and transfers were recorded once on each appropriate account.
File supporting documents in an organized location with sensible access controls and backups.
Avoid waiting until the tax deadline to make the first review. Small monthly corrections are usually easier to understand
than an entire year of unidentified activity.
Decide what to handle and what to delegate
An owner can remain responsible for approving payments and reviewing results while a bookkeeper handles data
entry, reconciliations, and reporting. A qualified accountant or tax professional may be needed for tax positions, formal
accounting judgments, or specialized compliance matters. Decide who owns each part of the process and document
the handoffs.
Paper Trail supports recurring bookkeeping and cleanup needs. The purpose is to maintain records the business can
actually use, with clear reporting, consistent documentation, and timely identification of unresolved transactions.
