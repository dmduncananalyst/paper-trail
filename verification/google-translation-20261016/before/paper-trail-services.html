Service Scope

Bookkeeping repair, often called bookkeeping cleanup, finds and corrects problems in existing business records. It can involve catching up missing months, removing duplicate transactions, resolving account differences and rebuilding reports from supporting information. Paper Trail begins with the condition of your records, then identifies the work needed to make them more useful.

Bookkeeping Process

Records Review

A growing list of uncategorized transactions is different from a bank account that has not reconciled in two years. A tax preparer requesting missing reports is different again. The first review looks at the periods involved, the accounts in use and the specific numbers that concern you. That creates a defined repair scope instead of treating every set of books as a complete rebuild.

Existing entries and past reconciliation reports can provide a useful starting point. The objective is to preserve reliable work and investigate the gaps.

Bookkeeping Scope

Past due bookkeeping addresses months that were never completed. Balancing and corrections focuses on records that exist but contain inconsistencies. Year end bookkeeping reviews the closing period and unresolved balances. Tax ready bookkeeping organizes the information your tax professional needs to review the business. More than one service may be appropriate, but they solve different parts of the problem.

  • Missing months need a sequence for catching up.
  • Wrong balances need an explanation and a supported correction.
  • A year end handoff needs complete reports and visible unresolved items.

Account Records

Repair work looks beyond the description imported by a bank feed. A deposit might represent a customer payment, an owner contribution or a transfer from another business account. A withdrawal might settle an existing bill instead of creating a new expense. These distinctions affect the reports, so transactions need context before they can be placed correctly.

Where an entry cannot be supported, it belongs on a clear follow up list. Guessing at its purpose can make the numbers look finished while leaving the underlying problem unresolved.

Service Coordination

Illustrative example: a business enters a supplier bill for $800, records its payment and then adds the downloaded bank payment as another expense. The books now show $1,600 of cost even though the supplier received $800. A supported correction removes the duplicate effect and connects the real payment to the original bill.

The important result is not simply a lower expense total. It is a record that explains what was purchased, when it was paid and why the remaining balance is correct.

Financial Reports

Useful starting information includes the bookkeeping software or spreadsheet in use, the last period you believe was completed and the number of business accounts involved. Once work is agreed, statements and relevant supporting documents can be requested through an appropriate secure method. You do not need to diagnose the accounting problem before describing what looks wrong.

If an earlier accountant made closing entries, those entries should be understood before changes are made. Corrections affecting a filed tax year may need coordination with your tax professional.

A report is easier to review when its balances can be traced to a statement or supporting schedule. Identify the reporting period, the accounts included and the information still missing. That separates an unexplained number from a balance that has actually been checked.

Keep the original report and the revised version with the correction record. Comparing the same reporting dates and accounting basis helps show the effect of a change without confusing it with activity from a different month.

Where a missing statement or an unanswered transaction question affects the figures, identify the limitation with the report. The owner and tax professional can then see which information is supported and which part of the review remains open.

Records Handoff

The repair scope should identify which periods and accounts will be reviewed, which reports will be refreshed and how remaining issues will be documented. Completed work should be distinguishable from items still awaiting a receipt, an explanation or an accountant decision. That makes the handoff useful to you and to the person maintaining the books afterward.

Repair is also a chance to identify the source of repeat problems. A confusing payment workflow, overlapping imports or inconsistent categories may need a simpler routine so the same issue does not reappear next month.

Paper Trail can organize a correction summary around the affected accounts, explain which periods were reviewed and separate completed changes from items awaiting approval or an accountant’s direction. This gives ongoing bookkeeping a documented starting point and helps your tax professional understand why a balance changed.

A repair project can involve one owner’s accounts or a larger business with several bank accounts, cardholders and payment channels. The review should identify transaction volume, the periods affected and how the accounting file connects with sales and payment records. Business size alone does not describe the problem. A defined scope makes the work, responsibilities and reporting output clear for everyone involved.
