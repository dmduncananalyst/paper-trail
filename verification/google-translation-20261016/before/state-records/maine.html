Maine Bookkeeping

Maine has relatively detailed records rules and a six year retention period for many state tax records. Accurate bookkeeping connects transactions to the invoices, receipts, bank activity, payroll records, and tax filings that rely on them. The details below explain the records that matter in Maine.

Keeping records that support the books

Maine Revenue Services Rule 103, Recordkeeping and Retention, requires every taxpayer, including every retailer registered under Title 36, chapter 211 and every service provider registered under the service provider tax law, to maintain all records necessary to determine correct tax liability and to make them available on request by the State Tax Assessor under Title 36, the recordkeeping section12(4). Source: Rule 103 text, https://www.maine.gov/future/sites/maine.gov.revenue/files/inline-files/Rule_103_July_2021.pdf

How state taxes affect the ledger

Maine has a sales tax at 5.5 percent on tangible personal property and certain taxable services, and a complementary use tax. Maine also imposes a service provider tax on specific services, including certain healthcare, community support, home support, and telecommunications and media services, which must be shown separately on a customer invoice and labeled as service provider tax, and cannot be combined with sales tax even on the same invoice. Source: Maine Revenue Services business guide, https://www.maine.gov/revenue/sites/maine.gov.revenue/files/inline-files/BusinessGuide07152022.pdf

Higher or specific rates apply to certain categories, notably lodging at 9 percent and prepared food and liquor in eating and lodging places, which require rate specific ledgers. Maine also has individual and corporate income taxes, employer withholding, and excise taxes on fuel, tobacco, and cannabis. Maine has no general local sales tax and no statewide gross receipts tax.

Payroll records and employer reporting

New hire reporting. Maine and federal law require new hires to be reported within 7 days of the date of hire or rehire, to the Division of Support Enforcement and Recovery through the Maine New Hire Reporting Program. This 7 day period is shorter than the 20 day period used in most states in this batch. Employers reporting more than 25 new hires must use an electronic option. Source: Maine Department of Health and Human Services, New Hire Frequently Asked Questions, http://www.maine.gov/dhhs/ofi/programs-services/child-support-services/employers/new-hire-faq

Unemployment insurance and withholding. Employers register with the Maine Department of Labor for unemployment insurance, file quarterly wage reports through its system, and register with Maine Revenue Services for withholding. Payroll records must support wages by employee by quarter.

How long to retain records

Title 36, the recordkeeping section35 requires persons subject to tax under Title 36 to maintain records as the State Tax Assessor determines necessary. Records for most taxes under Title 36 must be retained for at least 6 years. Records for certain fuel tax and International Fuel Tax Agreement reports must be retained for 4 years, and records for taxes under chapters 371 and 575 and Part 8 must be retained as long as required by applicable federal law and regulation. Source: statute text reproduced in Maine Revenue Services materials, https://www1.maine.gov/REVENUE/othertaxes/tobacco/ExciseBull3%20CigTob%20072009.pdf

When an industry needs additional records

Lodging, meals, and rentals. Operators must keep records by rate category, because lodging, prepared meals, and certain rentals are taxed at rates different from the general 5.5 percent rate, and service provider tax records must be kept for services listed in the state tax section. The business guide states that all service provider return records, including gross, exempt, and taxable services by category, must be retained for six years.

Healthcare and community support services. Providers subject to service provider tax must keep records of taxable and exempt services, including services for resale, services to exempt organizations, and services sold to out of state customers, by category.

Bookkeeping in practice

The following situations illustrate how a business might organize its records. They are examples, not a determination of tax treatment for any particular business.

A Maine inn charges for rooms and prepared meals. It keeps separate ledgers for lodging at its rate, meals at their rate, and retail goods at 5.5 percent, with all invoices and register tapes retained for six years, so each line on its sales tax return can be traced.

A home support provider subject to service provider tax lists that tax separately on invoices, labels it as service provider tax, keeps a record of exempt services to out of state customers, and retains the service provider return records for six years.

Longer retention and different taxable service categories

In Maine, an archive process should account for the six year rule in the cited state research, rather than automatically deleting older invoices after three years. Businesses selling meals, lodging, and other items should also avoid placing every kind of receipt in one undifferentiated tax category.

This is an accounting workflow distinction, not a claim that every business in Maine is subject to the same tax or filing rule. The applicable treatment depends on the actual transaction and the business activity. A bookkeeper's responsibility is to preserve the transaction evidence and classify the amounts consistently. Determining a disputed tax position or filing treatment is a separate professional judgment.

A practical monthly review for Maine businesses

Begin with the original source rather than the bank feed. A customer invoice, sales report, payroll register, supplier bill, or contract explains what occurred. Match it to the corresponding deposit or payment, investigate differences, and record adjustments with a clear explanation. When a state tax or payroll report is required, maintain a separate reconciliation from the ledger to the amounts included in that report. That allows an owner to compare operating results without confusing business income with taxes collected, employee withholdings, or other liabilities.

For an owner preparing to hand records to a tax professional, the useful package includes a period summary, filed return copies where applicable, the transactions supporting material adjustments, and a short list of unresolved questions. A reconciled bank account is only part of that package. The quality of the records depends on whether a reviewer can trace important figures back to their sources.

What to keep available

Maintain organized copies of the invoices, receipts, bank and card statements, payroll reports, account reconciliations, and any exemption or special program documents relevant to the business. A retention schedule should identify which law or program controls each category instead of assuming one deadline covers every record. Keep documents connected to a pending audit, claim, or dispute until the matter is resolved, and verify any destruction decision against the current applicable rule.
