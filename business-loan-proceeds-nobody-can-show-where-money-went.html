You Took a Business Loan and Now Nobody Can Show Where the Money Went

Published 11/12/2026

This article is general information about business recordkeeping, not tax or legal advice for a specific business.

The day the loan funded felt like relief. The deposit was large and unmistakable in the account, and for a moment the pressure that had been building for months eased. Rent was current, a supplier who had been waiting was paid, payroll felt less tight, and the business could breathe again. Nobody thought of the deposit as the start of a story that would have to be told, in full, months later. It felt like money that had arrived to do a job, and then it simply went to work.

That is exactly how it disappears as a separate thing. A loan does not stay in an account wearing a label. It mixes with customer payments, with the balance that was already there, with the ordinary flow of a busy month. It pays for things that look, in the bank feed, exactly like things paid from any other money. A parts order, a repair, a tax payment, a week of wages. Each payment is real, each one was needed, and none of them, on their own, announces that loan money was the source. By the second month, the loan is no longer a pile of money anyone can point to. It is a balance owed, on one side of the books, and a history of ordinary spending, on the other, with no clear thread tying the two together.

The trouble does not arrive while the money is being spent. It arrives when someone asks, calmly and reasonably, where it went.

The question sounds simple, and that is the problem

A lender, an accountant, or a partner asking about a loan is not asking a trick question. The loan agreement exists, the funding date exists, the amount exists. What they want is the middle of the story. The money came in, it was used for the business, the business still owes the balance, and the records should show that arc without guesswork.

In too many businesses, they cannot. The books may show the loan as income in the month it landed, which makes that month look stronger than it was. Or they may show the deposit and then lose it, with repayments recorded as ordinary expenses, which makes later months look weaker and stranger than they were. Or the loan may sit correctly as a liability while the spending it funded is scattered across categories with no notes, no receipts kept together, and no way for a reader who was not there to follow it.

The IRS recordkeeping guidance makes the underlying expectation plain. Businesses need records that support income, deductions, and credits, and supporting documents for purchases and expenses should identify the payee, the amount, proof of payment, and the date, along with what was bought. A loan tests that expectation in a particular way, because the most important fact about the money, that it was borrowed and must be repaid, is invisible in a bank deposit alone. A deposit from customers and a deposit from a lender can look identical to anyone reading the account later. Only the records around them carry the difference.

When those surrounding records are thin, the conversation changes tone. The owner knows the money went into the business. The owner was there, remembers the tight weeks, remembers what felt urgent. But the person asking was not there, and their job is not to grade intentions. Their job is to read what can be shown.

Ordinary spending is the hardest spending to trace

There is a quiet irony owners feel sharply in this moment. If the loan had bought one large piece of equipment, the story would be easier to follow. One invoice, one payment, one asset that still stands in the building as evidence. Most loans do not live that cleanly. They fund the ordinary life of a business that was short on cash, and ordinary life is dozens of payments, in familiar amounts, to familiar names.

Rent paid from loan money looks like rent paid from any money. Payroll run in a loan funded week looks like every other payroll. Inventory bought to keep shelves full, fuel, insurance, a contractor who had to be paid before a job could finish, each charge blends into the pattern around it. Months later, reconstructing which dollars were borrowed dollars is not merely difficult. In a shared account, it is a question the records were never set up to answer, and memory fills the gap with a confidence that fades under follow up questions.

That fading matters because the amounts rarely line up neatly by then. Part of the loan may still be sitting in the account, mixed with newer receipts. Part may have covered an overdraft or a credit card balance that itself covered earlier spending, so the trail doubles back on itself. Interest and fees add their own entries, small enough to ignore in a busy week and large enough, over a year, to make balances quietly disagree.

The owner ends up telling three versions without meaning to. The bank tells one, the loan statement tells another, and the books tell a third. None of them is dishonest. They simply were never made to agree while the story was still fresh.

The balance owed starts to feel like an argument

A loan that cannot be traced does not stay a private confusion. It follows the business into rooms where trust is being priced. An accountant preparing a return needs to know that the deposit was not revenue, because treating borrowed money as earned money distorts the year and everything built on it. A lender considering a second loan, a refinance, or a line increase reads the first loan as a test of stewardship, and a file that cannot show the use of funds asks that lender to extend more trust than the paper supports. A partner or co owner who was not part of the spending decisions hears explanations where they expected records, and even a fair explanation can start to sound like a story being built after the fact.

Tax time sharpens all of it. The federal baseline in the IRS guidance is that records supporting a return are kept until the limitations period for that return expires, generally three years from filing, longer in some cases, with employment tax records kept at least four years. A loan that spans a year end, as most do, lives inside that window on both sides of the calendar. The deposit in one year, the spending across two, the repayments still running. If the paper behind any leg of that journey is missing, the weakness is not contained to the month of the loan. It spreads across every return and report that touched the money.

Owners often describe the feeling as unfair, and in one sense it is. The money was not wasted in secret. It kept a real business running through real obligations, and the people who were paid could confirm it. But a loan is, by its nature, someone else money inside the business for a while, and that fact raises the standard for what the records need to show. Customer revenue belongs to the story the business tells about what it earned. Borrowed money belongs to a story about what it was trusted with. Confusing the two, even by neglect, is the point where a routine deposit becomes a credibility problem.

What remains when the trail goes cold

By the time anyone sits down to untangle it, the business is usually paying for the same loan twice. Once in the monthly payment itself, and again in the hours spent searching old statements, in the accountant time billed to reconstruct a year, in the delay while a lender waits for an answer that should have taken minutes. The deepest cost is the moment an owner realizes they cannot fully account for a significant amount of money that passed through their own business, and that everyone in the room can see it.

That moment rarely comes from one careless act. It comes from a funded loan treated, in the rush of relief, as if the deposit were the end of the paperwork instead of the beginning of it. The spending that followed was legitimate, and that legitimacy is exactly what let each payment pass without a second thought. Legitimate spending still needs a trail when the money was borrowed. Without one, legitimacy is a memory, and memory is not a record anyone else can rely on.

The loan will be repaid on schedule or it will not, and the statement will keep arriving with its calm, exact balance. The open question is whether their own books can stand next to that statement and tell the same story, in the same amounts, for the same months. When they cannot, the debt is not the heaviest part of the loan anymore. The missing story is.
