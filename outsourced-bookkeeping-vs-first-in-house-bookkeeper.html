Outsourced Bookkeeping vs. Hiring Your First In House Bookkeeper

Published 11/05/2026

When a business outgrows informal spreadsheets and occasional bookkeeping help, the owner often reaches a staffing decision. Should the company hire its first dedicated bookkeeper, or should it contract with an outside bookkeeping provider?

The choice is bigger than comparing one monthly price against a salary. It determines who maintains the books, how the business supervises the work, what happens when the responsible person is unavailable, and how financial records remain accessible as the company grows.

Define the Work Before Choosing Who Performs It

Start by identifying the tasks that actually need to be completed. Transaction categorization, account reconciliation, income and expense tracking, accounts payable and receivable records, and financial statement preparation are different responsibilities. Some companies also need regular invoicing, payment follow ups, or financial analysis.

A job description or service proposal should state which of those tasks are included, how often the work occurs, what information the business must provide, and when reports will be delivered. If one arrangement includes weekly reconciliations and another includes only a month end review, they are not equivalent offers.

A business should also separate bookkeeping from services that require specialized tax or legal expertise. Someone maintaining accurate records does not automatically become the company's tax preparer, auditor, or strategic finance executive.

What an In House Bookkeeper Changes

Hiring an employee can be attractive when bookkeeping is tightly integrated into daily operations. A person on staff may handle immediate internal questions, coordinate paperwork across departments, and work directly with managers as invoices and transactions arise.

That availability comes with management responsibilities. The employer needs to define the role, supervise quality, cover absences, maintain secure access, and plan for continuity if the employee resigns. Payroll taxes, employee benefits, recruiting costs, and training may also be relevant depending on the role and employment arrangement.

The IRS explains that worker classification depends on the actual relationship, including behavioral control, financial control, and the type of relationship. A business should not assume that calling someone an independent contractor makes that classification correct.

What Outsourcing Changes

An outsourced bookkeeping provider generally offers a defined service rather than a full time employee. The arrangement can give an owner recurring bookkeeping coverage without hiring a person to sit in the office. It can also provide a structured handoff and documented recurring tasks when the service agreement is clear.

But outsourcing does not remove the owner's responsibility to provide accurate information, answer questions about transactions, and review reports. The company still needs someone authorized to approve decisions, supply missing receipts, and explain unusual activity.

The provider's scope matters. Does the agreement cover every financial account or only selected ones? Are there monthly transaction limits? How are uncategorized transactions investigated? What happens when records are months behind? Does the service include invoices or bill coordination, or are those separately quoted?

Those questions prevent the business from buying a package based on a label while assuming it includes work the provider never agreed to perform.

Continuity and Account Ownership Matter in Either Arrangement

A bookkeeping relationship should never depend on one person's memory alone. The business needs clear ownership of its accounting software, bank records, invoices, supporting documents, and report history. It should understand how access can be transferred if the provider or employee changes.

A clean record of reconciled periods and outstanding questions makes the next month easier. If the relationship ends, the company should know what records it will receive, what remains incomplete, and which approvals are still needed.

The IRS says business records must support the amounts reported on tax returns. That ongoing obligation belongs to the business, regardless of whether an employee or outside provider maintained the books.

Make the Decision Based on Operational Fit

The right choice depends on the company's transaction activity, number of accounts, internal staffing, urgency of day to day questions, and appetite for managing an employee. Some organizations need an internal finance coordinator alongside an outside bookkeeper. Others are well served by a clearly defined recurring outsourced service.
