Read Your Balance Sheet Beyond the Bank Balance

A balance sheet shows what the business owns, what it owes and the owner’s recorded interest at a particular date. Its main groups are assets, liabilities and equity. It explains financial position, while a profit and loss statement explains performance over a period.

Read the three groups together

Assets can include bank cash, customer receivables, inventory and equipment. Liabilities can include vendor bills, card balances, loans and taxes payable. Equity reflects owner investment and accumulated results, with withdrawals or distributions affecting the balance. The accounting equation connects them, assets equal liabilities plus equity, but that mathematical balance does not prove every entry is correct.

Ask what can turn into cash and what must be paid

A large receivable balance may be less useful if customers are overdue. Equipment contributes to operations but usually cannot pay next week’s bills without being sold. Compare near term obligations with the assets reasonably available to meet them. Reports should be read with supporting schedules, such as unpaid invoice detail and lender statements.

Notice balances that need explanation

Negative receivables, an unexplained opening equity amount or a loan balance that never changes may point to recording issues. Some unusual balances are valid, so investigate before correcting them. The balance sheet is also not a market valuation of the business. Its usefulness comes from accurate records, a clear reporting date and an understanding of how the business’s resources and obligations are changing.
